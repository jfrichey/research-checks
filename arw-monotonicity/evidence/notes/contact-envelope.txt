2. A precisely scoped repair fails (PROOF + exact arithmetic)
-------------------------------------------------------------
Start from the independent product law mu x nu. A bad pair has target bits
(1,0); a good donor has bits (0,1). Exchanging the upper histories between
equal masses of these two pairs gives bits (1,1) and (0,0), preserving every
lower and upper marginal. This is a valid rectangle move on full histories.

Restrict the donor to a DIFFERENT joint contact pattern than the bad pair;
requiring opposite contact flags in both copies is even more restrictive.
Also require each move to consume a currently bad pair and a currently good
donor, with no preliminary rearrangement of the donor pool. Such moves
never create new bad pairs or good donors.

The initial bad demand with joint contact pattern (0,0) is m_01*n_00. Its
entire allowed donor capacity is sum_(b,c)!=(0,0) m_b0*n_c1. Demand exceeds
this capacity by

  614223302726662053931077841 / 37142523050367752227611355500
  (approximately .01653693).

Thus the proposed direct repair cannot fix all bad mass, even when donors
may come from ANY different joint contact pattern. This is a collective
capacity obstruction, not a claim about all possible multi-stage swaps.
Allowing same-pattern donors, reorganizing the pool, or changing the initial
coupling is outside this failed restriction. It illustrates F4's issue.

3. A general coarse-label coupling criterion (PROOF)
----------------------------------------------------
For any two complete-history laws define

  c_minus=mu(J=1), c_plus=nu(J=1),
  h_minus(b)=mu(I=1 | J=b), h_plus(b)=nu(I=1 | J=b).

Suppose 0<c_minus<=c_plus<1 and

  h_minus(0)<=h_plus(0),
  h_minus(0)<=h_plus(1),
  h_minus(1)<=h_plus(1).                                  (C)

There is an explicit coupling preserving BOTH COMPLETE HISTORY LAWS and
ordering the final target indicators:

(a) Couple contact flags using
    P(J_minus=0,J_plus=0)=1-c_plus,
    P(J_minus=0,J_plus=1)=c_plus-c_minus,
    P(J_minus=1,J_plus=1)=c_minus.

(b) Given flags (a,b), take one uniform U, and set
    I_minus=1{U<=h_minus(a)}, I_plus=1{U<=h_plus(b)}.
    Conditions (C) give I_minus<=I_plus for every permitted flag pair.

(c) Sample each complete history from its original law conditioned on its
    own (J,I), independently of the other history once these tags are set.
    Equivalently, reject independent complete histories until the requested
    tags occur. Every used tag has positive probability in this example.

Normalization proof: the flag marginal is the original c on each side;
conditional on a copy's own flag, its target bit has exactly its original
h, regardless of the partner's flag. Thus each copy's (J,I) marginal is
its original four-class table. Conditional sampling in (c) reconstructs
its full path law by disintegration. Any unread stack suffixes can retain
their correct conditional laws as well. No pointwise stack bijection is
claimed or needed.

This is a sufficient criterion for arbitrary history laws, not an ARW
monotonicity theorem. Its use in ARW requires proving (C). The finite
calculation below supplies (C) only for the stated example.

What changed relative to F2/F8: we never reveal the intermediate sleeping
support and then demand an unchanged conditional continuation law given
both copies' histories. We couple a coarse label of the COMPLETE history,
then fill in the history with explicitly normalized conditional laws. This
is a noncausal coupling within the complete {0,2} block. The individual
complete-history marginals are exact; the intermediate joint filtration is
different. There is no conflict with the previous posterior obstructions.

5. Why the simple contact weights appear (PROOF)
-------------------------------------------------
Until first contact with 0, only vertices 2 and 3 move. A lone active
particle at either vertex leaves toward its next vertex (2->3 or 3->0)
before sleeping with probability

  [(1/2)(1/2)] / [1-(1/2)(1/2)] = 1/3.

Below, site 3 starts empty, so hitting 0 requires both such departures:
c_minus=1/9. Above, site 3 initially has the extra sleeper. Once the seed
reaches 3, two particles there must eventually send a surplus particle to
0; self-loops only delay this. Thus c_plus=1/3. These probabilities do not
involve p_1 or the behavior after reaching 0.

This also gives an explicit interpretation of the conditional target
inequalities. Write g(c_0,c_1,c_2,c_3) for the eventual occupation
probability of 0 at the final parameters; -1 denotes a sleeper, 0 an empty
site, and positive integers active particles.

  h_minus(0) = (3/4) g(1,-1,-1,0) + (1/4) g(1,-1,0,-1),
  h_plus(0)  = g(1,-1,-1,-1),
  h_minus(1) = g(2,-1,0,0),
  h_plus(1)  = g(2,-1,0,1).

On the lower no-contact event, the seed either slept at 2 (unconditional
mass 2/3), or moved to 3 and slept there (mass 2/9); dividing by 8/9 gives
weights 3/4 and 1/4. On the upper no-contact event it must have slept at 2.
At first contact, the lower has two active particles at 0; the upper also
has one active particle remaining at 3. Site 1 has not yet been visited.
Independent exact absorption from these five displayed configurations
checks the conditional-probability formulas in the certificate.
